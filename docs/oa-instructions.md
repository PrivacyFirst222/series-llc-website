## 1. READ THIS FIRST — The Recordkeeping Warning
A Florida Protected Series LLC gives you two layers of liability protection. The first protects you personally from the company's debts. The second — the one that makes this structure special — walls off each protected series from the debts of the company and of every other series.
**That second wall exists only as long as your records exist.** Florida law (s. 605.2301, Florida Statutes) protects a series' assets only if the company keeps records specific enough that a "disinterested, reasonable individual" could tell exactly which assets belong to which series, when and from whom each asset was acquired, and — for anything moved between series — what was paid, by whom, and to whom. If those records are not kept, a creditor of one series can reach the assets of the company and of your other series, and Florida law places the burden of proving the records were adequate on **you**.
Article 8 of your operating agreement tells you exactly what to do. In practice it means:
**One bank account per series.** Never pay one series' expenses from another's account, or from your personal account.
**Title every asset in the full legal name of the series that owns it** — exactly as filed, for example: *[COMPANY NAME], LLC - PS 1*.
**Deposit each series' income to that series' account** and pay its expenses from that account.
**Document every asset moved between series** — what was transferred, the date, the price, who paid, and who received — at the time it happens.
**Keep the mothership (the company itself) asset-light.** Under §8.5(b) of your agreement, any asset you fail to associate with a series defaults to the company, where it is exposed to the company's creditors. Owning assets at the series level, with clean records, is the whole point of the structure.
**Review your records once a year.** Neither the statute nor your agreement requires it; do it anyway — it is the cheapest way to find a gap while it can still be fixed.
The free MyFloridaSeriesLLC recordkeeping app and the Series LLC User's Manual included with your formation package are built around these requirements.

## 3. Filling in the Blanks
Every blank appears in [BRACKETS]. Complete all of them:
**[COMPANY NAME]** — exactly as shown on your filed Articles of Organization, everywhere it appears, including in each Series Exhibit name.
**[DATE]** — the agreement's effective date.
**[PRINCIPAL ADDRESS]** — the company's principal office. This address also sets the courthouse for any dispute (venue is the county of the principal office).
**[MANAGER NAME]** — the manager. The manager does not have to be an owner.
**[MEMBER NAME(S)], addresses, percentages, contributions** — on Exhibit A. Multi-member percentages must total 100%.
**[THRESHOLD]** (multi-member §5.4(f)) — the dollar amount of debt the manager may incur without a member vote. Common choices: $10,000–$50,000 for small companies.
**[CAP]** (multi-member §6.2, if included) — see Section 5(a) below.
**Series Exhibits** — one per protected series. See Section 4.
## 4. Completing a Series Exhibit
Each protected series gets its own Series Exhibit (PS-1, PS-2, and so on), adopted when the series is designated. The series name must match the Sunbiz filing exactly — Sunbiz formats it as the company name, a space-dash-space, then the series identifier (for example, *[COMPANY NAME], LLC - PS 1*).
**The Asset Schedule attached to each Series Exhibit is the most important thing you will write.** The exhibit points to an attached Asset Schedule that you complete yourself — one per series, kept current as assets come and go. Describe each asset so a stranger could identify it without asking you anything:

| **Asset type** | **What to write** |
|---|---|
| Real property | Street address AND legal description; date acquired; who you acquired it from |
| Bank account | Institution, exact account title, last four digits of the account number, date opened |
| Vehicle | Year, make, model, VIN |
| Equipment | Description and serial number |
| Contract or lease | The parties and the date |
| Anything acquired from the company or another series | All of the above PLUS the price paid, who paid it, and who received it |

"Miscellaneous equipment," "various tools," or "the rental property" are **not** specific enough. If you cannot describe it, a court may treat it as unprotected.
## 5. Optional Provisions (Multi-Member Form)
The multi-member form contains three provisions marked **[OPTIONAL]** or **[SELECT ONE ALTERNATIVE]**. Decide before signing.
**How to omit a provision — never renumber.** When you omit an optional provision, do not delete the section number or renumber anything. Replace the section's text with the single word "[Reserved.]" — for example: *6.2 Additional Capital Contributions. [Reserved.]* Do the same for any cross-referenced item the option tells you to reserve (for example, *(a) [Reserved.]* in Section 11.1). This keeps every section number and cross-reference in the agreement accurate no matter which options you choose. The one exception is Section 4.7, where you keep the alternative you selected and delete the other alternative's paragraph entirely — the section number stays 4.7 either way. Also delete the bracketed instruction notes themselves (the italicized text in parentheses) from the final document.
**(a) Additional Capital Calls — §6.2.** If included, a majority of owners can require everyone to chip in more money (pro rata), up to the annual per-member **[CAP]** you set — a cap protects minority owners from being bled by repeated calls, so set it at a level everyone can actually pay (many owners choose a figure tied to a year of expected expenses for the properties involved). Including §6.2 does two things: it gives the company a funding mechanism, and it creates a continuing obligation that supports Article 11's bankruptcy protections (see Section 7). Omitting it means no owner can ever be forced to contribute more — friendlier to passive investors, but it removes one of Article 11's anchors and the company must then fund shortfalls through loans or new members. If you omit §6.2, replace the text of §6.2 and §6.3 with "[Reserved.]" and replace §11.1(a) with "(a) [Reserved.]".
**(b) Competition — §4.7, Alternative A or B.** Choose exactly one. **Alternative A** (no competing with the company or your own series while you are a member) suits active business partners who are each other's only allies — note it ends at dissolution and imposes nothing after an owner exits. **Alternative B** (competition permitted) suits passive co-investors, family arrangements, and anyone who invests in multiple ventures — real estate investors who own other rentals individually usually need Alternative B, or they would be breaching the agreement just by owning their other properties. If you select Alternative B, replace §11.1(c) with "(c) [Reserved.]".
**(c) Deadlock Buy-Sell ("Shotgun") — §13.2.** For companies that can split 50/50 (two equal owners, or two equal factions), a deadlock otherwise has no exit short of a lawsuit to dissolve the company. The shotgun works like cutting a cake: after a 60-day deadlock, either substantial owner may name a single price for the whole company; the *other* side then chooses whether to buy or sell at that price. Naming the price honestly is self-enforcing — name it too low and you get bought out cheap; too high and you overpay. **Caution:** the mechanism favors the owner with more cash, since the poorer side may be forced to sell even at a fair price. To omit it, replace the text of §13.2 with "[Reserved.]". Omit it if ownership is not evenly split (a majority can always outvote a deadlock) or if the owners' finances are badly mismatched.
## 6. Transfer-on-Death Designations
Both forms let each owner register a transfer-on-death (TOD) beneficiary on Exhibit A, using Florida's registration-in-beneficiary-form statute (ss. 711.50–711.512). At the owner's death, the designated beneficiary takes the interest directly — no probate — subject to the agreement. Any person or entity may be designated. In the multi-member form, a beneficiary who is a close family member of the deceased owner becomes a full member upon signing a joinder; any other beneficiary automatically receives the economic interest (distributions) but becomes a voting member only with the consent of a majority of the other members — death does not bypass the same controls that govern lifetime transfers.
Two things to understand: **(1)** the statute was written primarily for securities accounts; the agreement's language declaring the interest "registered" with the company is what brings membership interests within it, and while this design is used in practice, no Florida appellate decision has yet passed on it — owners with substantial estates should coordinate the TOD designation with their estate plan (a trust may serve the same purpose with more certainty); **(2)** to change a beneficiary, follow §4.11 (§4.6 in the single-member form) exactly — a signed writing, two witnesses, delivered to the manager. A will does not override a TOD registration.
## 7. The Executory Contract Article (Multi-Member Article 11)
Article 11 is bankruptcy armor. It frames the agreement as a contract with ongoing, unperformed duties owed by every member — capital calls (if §6.2 is included), participation in governance, noncompetition (if Alternative A is selected), confidentiality, and non-disparagement — and, citing Florida bankruptcy precedent, takes the position that a bankruptcy trustee for one member cannot take over or sell that member's interest without the other members' consent. Together with the charging-order provision (§10.6), it is designed to keep a co-owner's personal bankruptcy or judgment creditor from putting a stranger in your company.
Understand its limits: bankruptcy courts have broad equitable powers, and no provision of any operating agreement is guaranteed to control a bankruptcy court. Article 11 gives your side the strongest available argument; it is not a force field. Also note that the more optional duties you delete (capital calls, noncompetition), the fewer anchors Article 11 has — it still functions on the remaining duties, but each deletion narrows it.
## 8. Signing, Storing, and Changing the Agreement
**Sign before you operate.** The agreement (with its Series Exhibits) should be signed by every member and acknowledged by the manager before the company does business, and a Series Exhibit should be adopted no later than each series' designation is filed.
**Delete the draft footer** (the italicized line at the end of the form) and these instructions' cover references before signing. The signed agreement should contain only the agreement.
**Store the signed agreement with the company's records** — it is itself part of the records that protect your series (§8.5(a)).
**Amendments must be written and signed** — by the sole member (single-member form) or by all members (multi-member form; Series Exhibits amend with the consent of that series' associated members and the manager). Oral side-deals have no effect and undermine the structure.
**When to involve an attorney:** admitting outside investors, series operating in other states, an owner's divorce or bankruptcy, converting an existing LLC with existing debts, S corporation or other special tax elections, or any dispute. These forms are designed for common situations; they cannot substitute for advice about yours.
